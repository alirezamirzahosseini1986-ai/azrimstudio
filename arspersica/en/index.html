The gallery

An atlas of Persian craft

Vessels, jewellery, woodwork and textiles from the workshops of Iran. Each craft below is described by its materials, its makers and its history, so you know exactly what you are looking at.

Hand-hammered copper

Persian copperware is raised from a single sheet of copper, hammered cold, annealed in the fire and hammered again until the vessel takes shape. The small facets left by the hammer catch the light and also make the metal harder. Pots, trays, jugs and samovars are tinned inside so they can be used for food. Many pieces are finished with ghalamzani, fine engraving done with a chisel and a small hammer, showing flowers, birds and hunting scenes. Over the years copper darkens to a warm patina that collectors value as much as the shine.

History

Metalwork in Iran goes back thousands of years, and the copper bazaars of Isfahan, Kashan, Zanjan and Kerman still follow the old rhythm of the hammer. Under the Seljuks, workshops in Khorasan produced inlaid bronze and brass that are now treasured in museums. The coppersmiths’ bazaar, the mesgarha, remains one of the most recognisable sounds of a Persian city.

Minakari enamel

Minakari is the art of painting and firing coloured glass enamel onto metal, most often copper. The base is shaped by a coppersmith, covered with a white enamel ground and fired; the painter then adds the design in mineral colours, and the piece returns to the kiln several times. The typical palette is turquoise and cobalt blue with white, green and gold, used for flowers, birds and arabesques. Each layer must survive the heat, so a single plate can take many days. The finished surface is glossy, durable and keeps its colour for generations.

History

Enamelling was practised in Iran long before Islam, and the craft flourished again in Safavid Isfahan. In the twentieth century it was revived in Isfahan, notably by the master Shokrollah Sani’zadeh, and today the city’s workshops around Naqsh-e Jahan Square are its centre. In Persian, mina means the colour of the sky.

Firouzeh-kubi · turquoise inlay

In firouzeh-kubi, thousands of tiny pieces of natural turquoise are set side by side on a copper or brass vessel and fixed with a dark mastic, so the stone covers the surface like a mosaic. The metal shows only at the rims and in fine engraved bands. Each fragment is chipped and placed by hand, then the surface is ground and polished until it is smooth to the touch. The contrast between sky-blue stone and warm copper is what makes these vases, bowls and boxes so recognisable. Because natural turquoise varies, no two pieces share the same colour.

History

Persian turquoise, firouzeh, has been mined near Neyshabur in Khorasan for thousands of years and was traded along the Silk Road. Setting small stones onto metal became a speciality of Mashhad and Isfahan, and the technique is especially associated with the Qajar period. The word firouzeh is linked to firuz, ‘victorious’.

Malileh · silver filigree

Malileh is filigree: fine silver wire is twisted, flattened and bent into scrolls, flowers and lace-like frames, then soldered together under a small flame. A single pendant may contain hundreds of tiny curls, each placed by hand with tweezers. Zanjan’s makers create earrings, necklaces, brooches, bowls and small boxes that are surprisingly light for their size. The open work lets light and skin show through, which gives the jewellery its delicate, airy look. Good filigree is even, firmly soldered and has no visible joins.

History

Filigree is one of the oldest jewellery techniques in the Middle East, and in Iran the city of Zanjan has become its home, with workshops and bazaar shops devoted to malileh. Artisans often learn the craft within their families. Zanjan silver work is a well-known souvenir and wedding gift across Iran.

Turquoise & silver jewellery

Persian silversmiths set sky-blue turquoise cabochons in rings, pendants, bracelets and earrings, framing the stone with engraved or granulated silver. The most admired stones show an even, clear blue, and the best come from the Neyshabur mines. Each stone is cut and polished by hand to follow its natural shape, so settings are made one by one. Turquoise is traditionally worn as a stone of good fortune, and Persian rings are often given to mark journeys, weddings and births. Silver and turquoise together give a calm, timeless piece that suits everyday wear.

History

Neyshabur turquoise is among the oldest mined gemstones in the world and has been prized from ancient Persia to the courts of Europe. Mashhad, close to the mines, is the main market for cut stones and silver jewellery. Persian poets often use firouzeh as an image of the sky.

Khatam · marquetry

Khatam is a micro-mosaic in which thin rods of wood, bone, brass and sometimes camel bone are bundled into triangular patterns, glued, and sliced into thin sheets. These sheets are laid onto boxes, frames, chessboards and furniture, forming stars and polygons that repeat across the surface. A single square centimetre can hold dozens of tiny pieces. The finished object is sanded and lacquered, giving it a soft glow and a fine geometric rhythm. The work demands patience and precision at every step.

History

Khatam is associated above all with Shiraz and Isfahan and reached a high point under the Safavids, when it decorated palace doors and furniture. The craft continued through the Qajar era and is still taught in workshops today. The word khatam is often linked to inlay and the idea of fine setting.

Lalejin pottery

Lalejin is a small town near Hamedan whose workshops make thrown and glazed pottery: bowls, plates, jugs, tea sets and decorative tiles. The clay is shaped on the wheel, dried, decorated with simple painted motifs and covered with a glassy glaze, most famously a deep turquoise. Each piece is fired in a kiln, and small variations in colour and drip make every bowl unique. The ware is sturdy enough for daily use while still carrying the look of a traditional craft. On a table, Lalejin ceramics bring the colours of Persian tilework into the home.

History

Lalejin is often called the pottery capital of Iran, with a long tradition of family workshops. Persian ceramics more broadly have a deep history, from the lustreware of medieval Kashan, which gave the word kashi to tiles, to the glazed tiles of Isfahan’s mosques.

Pateh embroidery

Pateh is the hand embroidery of Kerman: woollen thread in many colours is stitched densely onto a woollen cloth until the base almost disappears. The motifs are paisleys (boteh), cypresses, flowers and medallions arranged in symmetrical fields. A large tablecloth can take months of work, and many pieces are made by women working at home. The result is soft, warm and richly coloured, used as tablecloths, runners, cushions and wall hangings. The density and evenness of the stitches show the quality of a piece.

History

Pateh grew out of Kerman’s long tradition of fine wool and shawl weaving, and it was traditionally part of a bride’s dowry. In recent decades organisations in Kerman have supported embroiderers so that the craft continues. The boteh motif in pateh is the same paisley found in Ars Persica’s logo.

Termeh

Termeh is a richly patterned woven fabric from Yazd, traditionally made from fine wool and silk on a draw loom. Its dense designs, above all small paisleys in deep red, ivory, green and gold, are woven into the cloth rather than printed or embroidered. On a hand loom only a few centimetres can be woven in a day, so handwoven termeh is rare and treasured. Today it is used for tablecloths, cushion covers and ceremonial covers, including for the Nowruz table. Its weight and soft sheen make it feel like a heirloom from the first day.

History

Termeh was prized at the Safavid and Qajar courts and was used for robes and fine furnishings. Yazd, a centre of silk and textile trade, remains its home. Traditional termeh weaving is part of Iran’s registered cultural heritage, and workshops in Yazd still keep the hand looms in use.

Rug guide

Questions we are asked most

How can I tell a hand-knotted Persian rug from a machine-made one?

Turn the rug over. On a hand-knotted rug the pattern is clearly visible on the back, the knots are slightly irregular and the fringe is the end of the warp threads, not a sewn-on trim. Machine-made rugs look perfectly uniform and often have a stiff backing. We explain the construction of every piece we offer.

What is the difference between a kilim and a carpet?

A carpet has a pile: rows of knots cut to an even height. A kilim is a flat-weave, made by interlacing coloured weft threads through the warp, so it has no pile, is lighter and is often reversible. Kilims suit kitchens, hallways, walls and sofas.

Are the colours natural?

Our weavers use natural, certified and safe dyes from plants and minerals such as madder, indigo and pomegranate. Natural dyes age gently and develop a soft patina over the years.

Can I see a rug before buying?

Yes. The collection is kept in Budapest. Book a viewing and we will prepare the pieces you are interested in, or we can show them to you by live video call.

Do you deliver outside Hungary?

Yes. Delivery anywhere in Europe can be arranged on request. Write to us with the piece and your address and we will send you the delivery options.

How do I care for a Persian rug?

Vacuum gently without a beater brush, turn the rug every few months so it wears evenly, keep it out of strong direct sunlight and blot spills at once with a clean cloth. For deep cleaning, choose a specialist hand-washing service.
