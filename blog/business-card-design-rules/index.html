Business Card Design: 7 Rules for a Card People Keep

17 November 20266 min readAlireza MirzahosseiniMagyarul

A business card is the smallest piece of design your business owns, and often the one people hold the longest. It goes into a wallet after a meeting, sits next to the till in a café, or ends up stuck on the fridge. When it looks sharp, it quietly says “this business is organised”. When the text is cut off at the edge or the phone number is too small to read, it says the opposite.

The good news: almost every problem we see on small-business cards comes from a handful of avoidable mistakes. Here are seven rules we follow when we design cards for clients, with one concrete fix for each.

1. Start with the size your printer actually prints

There is no single world standard. Vistaprint lists 85 × 55 mm as the usual size for the UK and Europe, 3.5 × 2 inches (88.9 × 50.8 mm) for the US and Canada, and 91 × 55 mm in Japan. Some European printers, such as Onlineprinters, also offer 90 × 50 mm. A design made for one size and squeezed into another ends up with cramped margins or awkward empty space.

The fix: choose your printer first, download their template, and design on that exact size. If you hand out cards in several countries, pick one size and stick with it, rather than resizing the layout every time.

2. Respect the bleed and the safe area

Printers cut cards from big sheets, and the cut can shift slightly. That is why print files have a bleed (extra background beyond the edge) and a safe area (a margin inside the edge where nothing important should sit). Onlineprinters, for example, asks for a 2 mm bleed on all sides of a 90 × 50 mm card and wants important information at least 4 mm from the trimmed edge.

The fix: extend every background colour or photo into the bleed, keep names, logos and numbers inside the safe area, and avoid thin borders hugging the edge — a tiny shift in cutting makes them look crooked.

3. Give each side one job

The most common mistake is trying to fit everything on the front: logo, slogan, five phone numbers, three social handles and a list of services. Nothing stands out, so nothing gets read.

The fix: let the front carry the brand — logo, maybe a short line about what you do — and use the back for contact details. Put them in a clear order: name, role, then the one or two ways you actually want people to reach you. If you rarely answer email, don’t make it the biggest line on the card.

4. Make the small text readable

Contact details are usually the smallest text on the card, and the most important. MOO recommends keeping all text at 8 pt or larger and making fine lines at least 0.5 pt thick; Onlineprinters, for example, sets 6 pt as its absolute minimum. Thin, light-grey script fonts on a dark background are the usual casualties.

The fix: set phone numbers and addresses at 8–9 pt in a simple, sturdy typeface with good contrast against the background. Print a test page at 100% and read it at arm’s length — if you squint, your customers will too.

5. Send a file that is ready for print

A card that looks perfect on screen can print with blurry photos, shifted colours or missing fonts. Printers’ guidelines are consistent here: MOO asks for artwork as a vector PDF and photos at 300 dpi or higher, and Onlineprinters asks for CMYK colour and for fonts to be embedded or converted to outlines.

The fix: export a print-ready PDF in CMYK with bleed included, fonts embedded, and any photos at 300 dpi at their printed size. Keep your logo as a vector file so it stays crisp. If your brand colour is important, ask the printer for a proof before ordering hundreds.

6. Use a QR code that leads somewhere useful

A QR code can save someone typing, but only if it points to the right place. A code that opens a generic homepage is a missed opportunity.

The fix: link to the one action you want after the meeting — a WhatsApp chat, a booking page, your menu or your Google review page. Use a link you control, so you can update it later without reprinting. Keep the code inside the safe area with clear white space around it, make it big enough to scan comfortably, and test it with a few different phones before you order.

7. Let the paper match the brand

People judge a card with their fingers as well as their eyes. A flimsy, glossy card can undercut a premium salon or restaurant brand; a heavy, textured stock with a simple design can make a small studio feel established. The finish should fit what you sell, not just what is cheapest.

The fix: order a small run or a sample pack first and compare papers in your hand. Choose one stock and finish, and use the same one every time you reprint, so your cards stay consistent.

Before your next reprint

Take your current card and check it against these seven rules. Most cards need two or three changes, not a full redesign: a cleaner back, bigger contact details and a print-ready file often make the biggest difference.
