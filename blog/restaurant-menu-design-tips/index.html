Restaurant Menu Design: 7 Fixes That Help Guests Order

24 November 20266 min readAlireza MirzahosseiniMagyarul

Your menu is the one piece of marketing every single guest reads. It is in their hands for several minutes, right at the moment they decide what to spend. Yet in many cafés and restaurants it is the most neglected design in the building: a Word document from opening day, prices crossed out with a pen, and dish names that only the kitchen understands.

A good menu does not need tricks. It needs to be easy to read, easy to choose from and consistent with the rest of your brand. Here are seven fixes we look at first when we redesign a menu, each with one thing you can do this week.

1. Design for how people actually read

You may have heard of the “sweet spot”: the idea that eyes jump to the top right of a menu, so that is where your most profitable dishes should go. When researcher Sybil Yang at San Francisco State University tested this with an eye-tracking scanner in 2012, she found no sweet spot. Most people read the menu in order, like a book, and slowly, as if they were reading for information. The part that got the least attention held restaurant information and a list of salads.

The fix: stop hiding things in “magic” corners. Put your sections in the order a meal happens — starters, mains, desserts, drinks — and give each dish a clear, readable line. If guests read carefully, clarity is the thing that pays off.

2. Build the menu around the main course

In the same study, Yang noted that guests seemed to choose a main dish first and then build the rest of the meal around it. That is useful: the main course is the anchor, and everything else is a natural add-on.

The fix: make the mains section the easiest to scan, with clear category headings (meat, fish, vegetarian). Then help the add-ons along: a short line such as “goes well with” under a main, or a small sides list right next to it, so guests do not have to flip pages to finish their order.

3. Drop the currency sign and the price column

In a 2009 Cornell study run at a café at the Culinary Institute of America, 201 diners were randomly given menus with prices as “$20”, “20” or “twenty dollars”. Guests whose menu showed plain numbers without the dollar sign spent on average $5.55 more — about 8 percent. The study only tested dollars, so treat this as a reasonable idea to try with forints or euros, not a proven result.

The fix: write prices as simple numbers (“3900” instead of “3900 Ft”) and explain the currency once, for example in a small line at the bottom. Many designers also suggest placing each price right after the dish description instead of in a neat column on the right, so guests choose by dish, not by scanning down a list of prices.

4. Make it readable in your real lighting

A menu that looks elegant on a bright screen can be hard to read on a candle-lit table or a dark bar counter. Thin script fonts, grey text on black, and long lines of small capitals are the usual problems.

The fix: print one test page and read it at a table during evening service. If you have to tilt it towards the light, increase the text size and contrast. Use one font for headings and one for text, and keep the decorative font for the logo and section titles only.

5. Describe dishes in plain, tasty words

Guests choose with their imagination. “Chicken breast, rice” tells them nothing; a paragraph of chef language tells them too much. Most dishes need one short line: the main ingredient, how it is cooked, and the one detail that makes it yours.

The fix: rewrite every description to 8–15 words and read them out loud. If a word needs explaining (a Hungarian classic, a regional cheese), add a few words in brackets. This matters even more for tourists who meet dishes like lecsó or túrós csusza for the first time.

6. Treat allergen information as part of the design

Under EU Regulation 1169/2011, restaurants and cafés must provide allergen information for food that is not prepacked. The regulation covers 14 main allergens, including gluten, peanuts, tree nuts, milk and soy (the WKO guidance below lists all 14). The format can vary by country: guidance from the Austrian Economic Chamber, for example, describes both written options (on the menu, a price list or a sign) and verbal ones backed by a written notice.

The fix: check the rules with your local food authority, then design the information in instead of taping it on later. A small number or letter code next to each dish, with a clear key at the bottom of the page, keeps the menu clean and helps guests with allergies order with confidence.

7. Keep print, QR and online menus the same

Many businesses now have three menus: the printed one, the QR code version and whatever is on Google, Instagram or a delivery app. When prices and dishes differ between them, guests notice, and it can cost you their trust.

The fix: keep one master file and update every version from it on the same day. Use the same fonts, colours and section names everywhere, so the menu feels like part of your brand. In a city like Budapest, a clear English version next to the Hungarian one is not a nice extra — it is what lets visitors order without asking for help.

Before you reprint

Take your current menu to a table, sit where a guest sits and read it from start to finish. Most menus do not need a full redesign: clearer sections, simpler prices, better contrast and short descriptions usually make the biggest difference.
