Restaurant Menu Design: 6 Fixes for a Menu That Works Harder

10 November 20266 min readAlireza MirzahosseiniMagyarul

Your menu is the one piece of marketing almost every guest reads. It is in their hands at the exact moment they decide what to spend, and it works every service, whether you are watching or not. Yet in many cafés and restaurants it is the most neglected design in the building: a Word document printed years ago, prices patched with stickers, and a QR code that opens a blurry PDF.

You don’t need a big redesign to get more from it. Here are six practical fixes, based on research and on solid design practice.

1. Design for how guests really read

You may have heard of the menu “sweet spot” — the idea that eyes go first to the upper right of a two-page menu, so that is where your most profitable dish should sit. But in an eye-tracking study reported in 2012, hospitality researcher Sybil Yang found no evidence of a sweet spot. People read menus sequentially, like a book: left to right and down the page. Her conclusion was good news for owners: guests read menus carefully.

The fix: stop hunting for a magic corner and build a clear order instead. Arrange sections in the order of a meal (breakfast or starters first, desserts and drinks last) and put the dish you most want to sell first in its section, where a reader meets it before anything else. If a dish deserves extra attention, give it a short box or a little more space — one per page, not five.

2. Let the food lead, not the price

In a 2009 Cornell University study at a restaurant of the Culinary Institute of America, researchers gave 201 diners menus with prices written in different ways. Guests whose menus showed plain numerals with no dollar sign spent on average about 8 percent more than guests whose menus used a dollar sign or wrote the price out in words. It is one study, in one restaurant, but the idea is cheap to test: the less your menu looks like a price list, the more guests think about the food.

The fix: write prices simply (“2490” rather than “2.490,- Ft” on every line). We’d also suggest putting each price at the end of its description rather than in a neat column down the right-hand side, and avoiding dotted lines that pull the eye straight to the numbers. Prices still have to be clear and unambiguous — in Budapest, a bare number could even be mistaken for euros — so check your local price-display rules and state the currency once, clearly, for example “All prices in HUF, including VAT”, along with any service charge.

3. Fewer dishes, better descriptions

A long menu feels generous to the owner and exhausting to the guest. Every extra line is something a hungry person has to read past, and something your kitchen has to stock. Vague names don’t help either: “Chicken salad” tells a guest nothing; “Grilled paprika chicken, crunchy lettuce, lemon-yoghurt dressing” makes them hungry.

The fix: go through your sales and remove the dishes that rarely sell and are hard to make. Then give every remaining dish one honest, specific line: the main ingredient, how it is cooked and one detail that makes it yours. Keep descriptions short enough to read in a few seconds.

4. Make it readable at a dim table

Many menus fail for simple physical reasons: text that is too small, thin fonts, low contrast on dark paper, or a busy background photo. Remember who is reading — often at night, in warm low light, sometimes without their glasses. In Budapest, a large share of your guests may also be visitors who don’t read Hungarian.

The fix: use one or two clean typefaces, a comfortable text size and strong contrast. Test a printed proof in your own dining room in the evening, not on a bright office screen. If you serve international guests, give each dish its English name directly under the Hungarian one, in a lighter style, instead of printing a separate menu that quickly goes out of date.

5. Show allergens clearly

This one is not optional. Under EU Regulation 1169/2011, food businesses must provide information on 14 specific allergens for food that is not prepacked, including meals served in restaurants and cafés. Ireland’s Food Safety Authority, for example, explains that in Ireland it must be given in writing where the food is presented, sold or supplied. The exact form can depend on national rules, so check what applies where you operate. Done well, it also builds trust: guests with allergies remember the places that make ordering easy.

The fix: give each allergen a small number or letter, mark it next to every dish, and add a short key at the bottom of each page. Keep the same system on your printed menu, your website and your delivery platforms, and update it whenever a recipe changes.

6. Keep your online menu in sync with your printed one

Many guests choose you before they arrive, by checking your menu on Google, Instagram or your website. If that menu is a phone photo of an old version, or a PDF that needs zooming, you may lose them before they sit down.

The fix: publish your menu as a simple, mobile-friendly web page with the same sections, names and prices as the printed version, and link it from your Google Business Profile (we covered that in our Christmas checklist). For seasonal specials — a winter menu, Christmas dishes, mulled wine — use a separate insert or a single page you can swap, so the core menu stays clean.

Start with one evening

Pick a quiet evening, sit at one of your guest tables and read your menu like a first-time guest. Is the order clear? Can you read it in that light? Do the prices shout louder than the food? Fix the two biggest problems first; most of these changes cost little more than a reprint.
